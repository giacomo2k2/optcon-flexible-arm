\section{Introduction: The Issue with Long Horizon}
In the field of nonlinear trajectory optimization, particularly when applying Newton-type methods to discrete-time systems,
fundamental instability is often observed when attempting to generate long-horizon trajectories ($T \upuparrows$) directly from scratch, mainly involving a 
settling to unstable equilibria. 

This phenomenon, often referred to as the \textit{instability of the Single Shooting method}, is a direct
 consequence of the exponential propagation of sensitivities through the system dynamics. The optimization problem aims to find a control sequence $\mathbf{u}$ 
 that minimizes a cost function subject to the discrete dynamics:
\begin{equation}
    x_{t+1} = A_t x_t + B_t u_t
\end{equation}
where $A_t$ and $B_t$ are the Jacobians of the nonlinear possibly time-varying dynamics at time $t$. The conditioning of the resulting optimization problem is intrinsically 
linked to the spectral properties of the evolution of these matrices.

\section{Sensitivity Analysis and the Evolution Matrix}

The primary driver of numerical explosion is the sensitivity of the final states (e.g $x_N$) to initial control inputs. 
Expanding the discrete dynamics recursively, the state at time $N$ (analogously for any state time $t$) can be expressed as a function of the initial state $x_0$ and the control history:
\begin{equation}
    x_N = \left(\prod_{k=0}^{N-1} A_k\right) x_0 + \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} \left( \prod_{j=i+1}^{N-1} A_j \right) B_i u_i
\end{equation}
Since the optimization relies on gradients and Hessians derived from these sensitivities, the term $\prod_{k=0}^{N-1} A_k$ plays a critical role.
Since each $x_t$ is plugged into the cost function, it is clear how the shape of the cost landscape is heavily influenced by matrices linearizations of the system.
For example, for a linear time-invariant (LTI) approximation where $A_k \approx A$, the sensitivity of the state $x_N$ to an early control input $u_0$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial x_N}{\partial u_0} = A^{N-1}B
\end{equation}
If the system possesses unstable modes (eigenvalues of $A$ with magnitude $|\lambda| > 1$), the term $A^{N-1}$ grows exponentially with the horizon length $N$.
Analogously, if we work with trajectories that get linearized around unstable equilibria, the local $A_t$ matrices will have eigenvalues outside the unit circle for the time they are close to those points, 
leading to similar exponential growth in sensitivity.

\subsection{Ill-Conditioning of the Hessian}
Newton's method updates the control sequence by solving a linear system involving the Hessian of the cost function, $H$. The step $\Delta \mathbf{u}$ is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \mathbf{u} = -H^{-1} g
\end{equation}
where $g$ is the gradient. As $N$ increases, the massive magnitude disparity in the sensitivity matrix $A^N$ causes the condition number of the Hessian, 
$\kappa(H) = \frac{|\lambda_{\max}|}{|\lambda_{\min}|}$, to skyrocket. This leads to the following effect: infinitesimal changes in the inputs (especially first ones) imply massive 
changes in $x_N$, causing the Newton step to overshoot local minima and resulting in numerical divergence.

\section{The Dwell Time Phenomenon}
Let's now try to imagine an experiment where we want to throw a ball on the top of a steep hill.
If we only care about reaching the top at time $T$, we can throw it with a certain initial velocity and eventually let it roll down the other side.
However, if we want the ball to stay balanced on the hilltop for a duration of time each $mN$ of force matters for the ball to stay perfectly balanced. The idea of planning the whole input sequence at the beginning is rather difficult.
Empirical observations indeed indicate that optimization is significantly more prone to failure when the target requires maintaining an unstable equilibrium for some time compared to simply reaching it at the terminal time.

\subsection{Dynamics of the Unstable Equilibrium}
Maintaining a state $x_{eq}$ where the local linearization $A$ has unstable eigenvalues requires the control input to perfectly cancel the natural divergence of the system at every time step (if it can!!!). 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Passing Through:} When targeting the equilibrium only at the final time $T$, the system spends the majority of the trajectory in the transient phase. The solver exploits the system's natural momentum, and the cumulative sensitivity product is not dominated by the unstable eigenspace for long durations.
    \item \textbf{Dwelling:} When forced to stay at the unstable equilibrium, any numerical error $\epsilon$ at some initial $t$ is amplified by $A^k$ over the dwell duration. For a mode with $\lambda = 1.01$ and a dwell of 300 steps, the error amplification is $1.01^{300} \approx 19.788$.
\end{itemize}
This creates a "narrow ridge" in the optimization landscape. The solver must find a control sequence that balances on this ridge; a slight deviation results in a massive penalty gradient, often pushing the variables into the numerical overflow region.

\section{System Theoretic Guarantees: Controllability and Stabilizability}

The ability of the Newton solver to find a solution is mathematically bounded by the structural properties of the system: Controllability and Stabilizability.

\subsection{The PBH Test}
While standard controllability checks the rank of the Kalman controllability matrix $[B, AB, \dots, A^{n-1}B]$, numerical conditioning is better assessed via the Popov-Belevitch-Hautus (PBH) test. A system is stabilizable if and only if:
\begin{equation}
    \text{rank}([\lambda I - A \mid B]) = n \quad \forall \lambda \in \mathbb{C}, \quad |\lambda| \ge 1
\end{equation}
In underactuated systems (our case), kinematic singularities can cause this rank to drop (in our case we control only through coupling!). If the rank deficiency occurs at an unstable eigenvalue, the mode is \textit{uncontrollable} and \textit{unstable}. In such configurations, the Hessian becomes singular, and the Newton step is undefined.

\subsection{The DARE and Terminal Cost}
Infinite-horizon stability is often approximated using the solution $P_{\infty}$ to the Discrete Algebraic Riccati Equation (DARE):
\begin{equation}
    P = A^\top P A - (A^\top P B)(R + B^\top P B)^{-1}(B^\top P A) + Q
\end{equation}
The existence of a unique positive semi-definite solution $P_{\infty}$ strictly requires that the pair $(A,B)$ be \textit{stabilizable} and $(A, Q^{1/2})$ be \textit{detectable}.
If the trajectory enters a region where local stabilizability is lost (i.e., the PBH test fails for an unstable mode), the DARE solution $P_{\infty}$ loses its guarantees to asimptotically stabilize the system. Consequently, the cost in the optimization may become effectively infinite, causing the solver to crash.

\section{Mitigation Strategy}

To circumvent the ill-conditioning caused by $A^N$ and ensuring valid linearization regions, a \textit{Horizon Extension} (or Continuation) strategy is employed.
Returning to our ball-on-hill analogy, instead of throwing the ball directly to the top and trying to balance it there, we first try to reach the target height at $T$.
Later, we incrementally extend the horizon, using the previously computed trajectory as a warm start for the next optimization. Basically, we are asking the task to stay just a bit longer each time.
This approach leverages three key advantages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Short Horizon Initialization:} Optimizing for a small $T$ ensures that the sensitivity matrix $A^T$ remains bounded. The Hessian is well-conditioned, and the basin of attraction for Newton's method is large.
    \item \textbf{Warm Starting:} The solution from the short horizon $T$ serves as the initial guess for $T + \Delta T$. This effectively "anchors" the trajectory in a feasible region of the state space.
    \item \textbf{Iterative Refinement:} As $T$ extends, the solver only needs to refine the control inputs locally. Since the trajectory is already close to physical feasibility, the quadratic approximation holds, and the solver avoids the explosive divergence associated with global search in ill-conditioned landscapes.
\end{enumerate}

This approach effectively conditions the "evolution matrix" of the optimization by solving a sequence of well-posed problems rather than a single, numerically hazardous one.
