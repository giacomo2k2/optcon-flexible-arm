\section{Task 2: Trajectory Generation (Newton's Method)}

To generate feasible trajectories transitioning between system equilibria, we employed a Newton-type method for optimal control.

\subsection{Reference Generation}
Given the non-convex nature of the optimization landscape, the solver requires a reasonable reference trajectory to guide convergence, even if it is dynamically infeasible. We analyzed two distinct generation methods:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Stepwise:} A naive approach involving an instantaneous switch from the initial state $x_{start}$ to the goal state $x_{goal}$. This introduces a large discontinuity in the error term, often leading to \textbf{poor convergence} or aggressive control spikes.
    \item \textbf{Polynomial Interpolation (Poly 5):} We generated smooth references for joint positions and velocities using 5th-order polynomials. This method ensures kinematic consistency by respecting boundary conditions (position, velocity), thereby significantly aiding the solver in finding a feasible solution.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Performance Analysis: Stepwise vs. Polynomial Initialization}

The convergence properties of Newton-type methods are highly sensitive to the quality of the initial reference trajectory. Our analysis indicates that the initialization strategy is a determining factor in the solver's ability to locate a feasible solution within the under-actuated dynamic constraints.

\subsubsection{Limitations of Stepwise Initialization}
Applying Newton's method to the \textbf{Stepwise} reference resulted in significant numerical instability. As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:newton_stepwise}, the instantaneous state transition implies an infinite velocity and acceleration requirement at the discontinuity. This generates a large localized error gradient, causing the solver to propose \textbf{impulsive control torques} ($u$). Such spikes often drive the system state into regions of the manifold where the linearization approximation breaks down, leading to divergence or stabilization at infeasible equilibria.

The results demonstrate that Newton's method successfully converges to accurate trajectories for upward maneuvers (Swing Up, Elbow Up). However, the algorithm struggles to generate valid trajectories for the Swing Down task, as evidenced by the deviation in Figure \ref{fig:newton_poly_swing_down}.

This dichotomy in performance is attributable to the fundamental difference in energy dynamics required for these maneuvers:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Energy Conversion (Upward Maneuvers):} In swing-up tasks, the control objective aligns with the \textbf{natural conversion of kinetic energy into potential energy}. As the links rise, gravity naturally decelerates the system. The polynomial reference effectively guides this energy transfer, requiring only corrective control efforts to maintain the path.
    
    \item \textbf{Energy Extraction (Downward Maneuvers):} In contrast, downward motions involve a \textbf{release of potential energy}, which accelerates the system. Due to the under-actuated nature of the second link and the low-friction environment, the system lacks a passive mechanism to dissipate this accumulated kinetic energy. The controller cannot brake the passive link directly; instead, it must actively extract energy from the system via dynamic coupling between the links. This requirement creates a complex, non-intuitive optimization landscape where simple polynomial initialization fails to provide a sufficient gradient towards the feasible solution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Optimization Challenges}
Applying the standard Newton method directly to complex, long-horizon maneuvers revealed several critical numerical and theoretical issues:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Non-Convex Optimization Landscape:} The trajectory manifold for complex maneuvers is highly non-linear and is riddled with local minima, even though cost is convex. If the initial guess falls within the basin of attraction of a local minimum, representing a partial swing or a stalled state, the \textbf{solver is unable to escape} to the local optimum that guarantees the target trajectory.
    
    \item \textbf{Long Time Horizons and Sensitivity:} Long time horizons $T$ introduce significant challenges for Newton-based trajectory generation. Due to the system's chaotic dynamics, the sensitivity matrix ($\nabla \phi(\textbf{u})$) diverges exponentially in norm when crossing unstable regions. This results in a high condition number for the shooting Jacobian, making the Newton's update steps numerically unstable and prone to divergence. 

    \item \textbf{Fixed Horizon Inflexibility:} A significant limitation of standard Newton's method is the rigidity of the \textbf{fixed time horizon}. If the chosen characteristic timescale $T$ is mismatched with the natural frequency of the maneuver, the optimization may be physically impossible to solve, leading to poor convergence.

    \item \textbf{Controllability and Terminal Cost:} Implementing a stabilizing terminal cost $Q_T$ requires solving the \textbf{Discrete Algebraic Riccati Equation (DARE)}. This necessitates the system linearization $(A, B)$ at the target equilibrium to be stabilizable. In under-actuated configurations, kinematic singularities can cause rank deficiencies in the controllability matrix, rendering the DARE unsolvable.

    \par While standard controllability checks the rank of the Kalman controllability matrix \\
    $[B, AB, \dots, A^{n-1}B]$, numerical conditioning is better assessed via the \textbf{Popov-Belevitch-Hautus (PBH) test}. A system is stabilizable if and only if:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{rank}([\lambda I - A \mid B]) = n \quad \forall \lambda \in \mathbb{C}, \quad |\lambda| \ge 1
    \end{equation}
    In underactuated systems like in our case, kinematic singularities can cause this rank to drop (In this system, controllability is achieved solely through dynamic coupling). If the rank deficiency occurs at an unstable eigenvalue, the mode is \textbf{uncontrollable} and \textbf{unstable}. In such configurations, the Hessian becomes singular, and the Newton step is undefined.

    \item \textbf{Instability to Dwell Time:} We observed numerical instability when requiring the system to maintain an \textbf{unstable equilibrium for a final time interval} (dwell time). Consider the analogy of balancing a ball on a sharp peak: reaching the peak at time $T$ requires precision, but remaining there requires perfect force cancellation over time.  Any numerical error $\epsilon$ at $t < T$ is amplified by the unstable eigenvalues of the state transition matrix ($A_t$) which reflects on $\prod_{i=t+1}^{T-1} A_t$. This creates a "narrow ridge" in the cost landscape where slight deviations result in massive penalties.

    \item \textbf{Linearization Validity:} The optimization relies on linearizing the non-linear dynamics around the current trajectory. If the update step is too large, the Taylor approximation breaks down, leading to valid descent directions becoming actual ascent directions in the true non-linear landscape.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Proposed Solution: Dynamic Horizon Extension}

To address the ill-conditioning and local minima problems described previously, we implemented a \textbf{Dynamic Horizon Extension} strategy. Instead of attempting to solve the optimization problem for the full duration $T_{final}$ immediately, we solve a sequence of sub-problems with \textbf{incrementally increasing horizon lengths}. This strategy effectively conditions the optimization by ensuring the solver always operates within a valid local approximation of the system dynamics.

\subsubsection{Theoretical Justification}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Conditioning:} For a short horizon $T$, the norm of the sensitivity matrix ($\prod A_t$) remains bounded. This results in a well-conditioned Hessian and a significantly larger basin of attraction for Newton's method.
    \item \textbf{Warm Starting:} The optimal solution derived for horizon $T$ serves as the initial guess for the extended horizon $T + \Delta T$. This "anchors" the subsequent optimization in a feasible region of the state space, maintaining proximity to physical constraints.
    \item \textbf{Iterative Refinement:} As the horizon expands, the solver's task is reduced to refining the control inputs locally rather than discovering a global solution from scratch. This prevents the explosive divergence typically associated with global searches in ill-conditioned landscapes.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Algorithm Implementation}
The iterative procedure is defined as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Initialize:} Set a short initial horizon $T_{curr} \ll T_{final}$ such that the DARE-based terminal cost effectively approximates the remaining cost-to-go.
    \item \textbf{Converge:} Execute the Newton solver until the optimality criteria (gradient norm close to zero) are met for the current $T_{curr}$.
    \item \textbf{Extend:} Use the computed trajectory $x^*(t)$ as the initial guess for the next phase. The horizon is extended by $\Delta T$, and the reference tail is padded with the target equilibrium. The initial guess for the new segment is generated by evolving the system dynamics forward from the previous terminal state.
    \item \textbf{Repeat:} Increment $T_{curr} \leftarrow T_{curr} + \Delta T$ and repeat the process until $T_{curr} \geq T_{final}$ and the terminal error satisfies $\|\mathbf{x}_T - \mathbf{x}_{target}\| < \epsilon$.
\end{enumerate}

This proves that the algorithm successfully transforms a difficult global search into a series of manageable local refinements.

\subsubsection{Convergence Analysis}
The results highlight a clear distinction in the complexity of the solution manifolds for upward versus downward motions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Upward Maneuvers (Swing Up, Elbow Up):} As observed in Figures \ref{fig:newton_extended_swing_up} and \ref{fig:newton_extended_elbow_up}, the optimizer converges to a feasible trajectory almost immediately within the initial iterations. This rapid convergence suggests that the trajectory manifold for these energy-injection tasks is well-behaved and exhibits a \textbf{quasi-convex} structure in the vicinity of the polynomial reference. The solver easily locates a path that flows naturally toward the target equilibrium without requiring extensive temporal adjustments.

    \item \textbf{Downward Maneuver (Swing Down):} In contrast, the Swing Down task (Figure \ref{fig:newton_extended_swing_down}) necessitates a substantial adaptation of the optimization problem. The algorithm autonomously extends the time horizon significantly, converging on a final duration approaching $T \approx 20\text{s}$. This behavior underscores the difficulty of the energy extraction phase; the system requires a longer time window to pump energy out via \textbf{dynamic coupling} while respecting actuation limits.
\end{itemize}

Crucially, this result validates the necessity of the incremental approach. A standard Newton method initialized with a fixed $20\text{s}$ horizon would inevitably diverge due to the severe ill-conditioning of the Hessian matrix and the accumulation of sensitivity errors over such a long duration. The Dynamic Horizon Extension avoids this by solving a sequence of well-conditioned sub-problems, allowing it to traverse a solution path that is inaccessible to the static solver.
