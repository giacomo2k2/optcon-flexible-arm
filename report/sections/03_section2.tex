\section{Task 1: Equilibria and Terminal Cost}

\subsection{Equilibrium Manifold}
The system's equilibria are defined by states where $\dot{q} = \ddot{q} = 0$. This implies the gravity and stiffness torques must be balanced by the input $\tau$.
We analyzed the dynamics equation at equilibrium:
\begin{equation}
    \cancel{M(\theta_1, \theta_2) \begin{pmatrix} \ddot{\theta}_1 \\ \ddot{\theta}_2 \end{pmatrix}} + \cancel{C(\theta_1, \theta_2, \dot{\theta}_1, \dot{\theta}_2) \begin{pmatrix} \dot{\theta}_1 \\ \dot{\theta}_2 \end{pmatrix}} + \cancel{F \begin{pmatrix} \dot{\theta}_1 \\ \dot{\theta}_2 \end{pmatrix}} + G(\theta_1, \theta_2) = \begin{pmatrix} \tau \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
This reduces to:
\begin{equation}
    \underbrace{
    \begin{pmatrix} 
    gl_{c1}m_1 \sin(\theta_1) + gm_2(l_1 \sin(\theta_1) + l_{c2} \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)) \\ 
    gm_2l_{c2} \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2) + 3k \sin(\theta_2) \cos^2(\theta_2) 
    \end{pmatrix}
    }_{G(\theta_1, \theta_2)} 
    = 
    \underbrace{
    \begin{pmatrix} \tau \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}
    }_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
    \vspace{0.2cm}
\end{equation}

In this framework, we have three variables: $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$, and $\tau$. By fixing one of the three variables, we can solve for the other two.
Given that the first joint is actuated, it is intuitive to fix $\theta_1$ and solve for $\theta_2$ and $\tau$, since we are interested in obtaining specific configurations.

The first thing we can see is that the manifold has a sinusoidal shape and a periodic behavior, which is expected given the rotational nature of the joints.
From the plot, we can also observe some important characteristics:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Branches:} We can say that the manifold is \textbf{composed of branches}, hence there will be starting and ending equilibria that are on the same branch and others that will be in two different ones.\\
This will have an impact on the trajectory generation, as moving between different branches may require \textbf{highly dynamical maneuvers} since it's not possible to switch branch by steady-state maneuvers.
\item \textbf{Uncontrolled second link:} Since the second joint is unactuated, the torque $\tau$ is only applied at the first joint.\\
This means that the system's ability to reach certain \textbf{configurations} in a given \textbf{time} is \textbf{limited by the dynamics of the second link} and the coupling between the two joints.
This coupling will prove useful in achieving a stabilization of the second link by appropriately controlling the first one. 
We will see this is \textbf{not always possible}, and this causes problems in trajectory generation when trying to reach certain target equilibria and maintain them for some time.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Cost Function}
For the optimal control problem, we defined the quadratic cost function:
\begin{equation}
J = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \left( \frac{1}{2} \Delta x_t^\top Q \Delta x_t + \frac{1}{2} \Delta u_t^\top R \Delta u_t \right) + \frac{1}{2} \Delta x_T^\top Q_T \Delta x_T
\end{equation}
where $\Delta x = x - x_{ref}$ and $\Delta u = u - u_{ref}$. We selected $Q = \text{diag}(1, 1, 1, 1) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_s \times n_s}$ and $R = 10^{-3}I \in \mathbb{R}^{n_i}$ to penalize state deviation while allowing for aggressive control actions.

\subsection{Terminal Cost via DARE}
A critical aspect of the finite-horizon optimization is the terminal cost $Q_T \in \mathbb{R}^{n_s \times n_n}$. Simply setting $Q_T = Q$ often fails to stabilize the system at the end of the horizon, especially for short durations.
In fact, without an appropriate terminal cost, the solver may produce trajectories that approach but do not settle at the desired equilibrium, leading to oscillations or drift post-optimization.
Hence, the optimizer behaves  \textbf{'myopically'}, focusing only on minimizing cost up to time $T$ without considering the long-term behavior beyond this point.
To address this, we computed $Q_T$ by solving the \textbf{Discrete Algebraic Riccati Equation} (DARE) based on the linearization at the final target equilibrium $(x_{eq}, u_{eq})$.

\begin{equation}
P_{\infty} = A^\top P_{\infty} A - (A^\top P_{\infty} B)(R + B^\top P_{\infty} B)^{-1} (B^\top P_{\infty} A) + Q
\end{equation}
Setting $Q_T = P_{\infty}$ provides an approximation of the infinite-horizon cost-to-go. This essentially informs the solver that the trajectory continues optimally after time $T$, significantly improving convergence and stability for tasks like the "Swing Up".

\subsection{Step Size Selection (Armijo Rule)}
To ensure global convergence of the Newton method, we implemented a \textbf{line search using the Armijo rule}. The step size $\alpha$ is reduced ($\alpha \leftarrow \beta \alpha$) until the actual cost reduction is a sufficient fraction of the predicted reduction (based on the gradient). 
This prevents the solver from taking overly aggressive steps that could destabilize the trajectory and \textbf{ensures steady progress towards the optimum}.
Additionally, the implementation includes a gradient check that validates the analytic descent direction by comparing it to a numeric slope $\frac{J(u + \epsilon \Delta u) - J(u)}{\epsilon}$ with $\epsilon \simeq 0$computed via finite difference. 
If the Armijo search fails to satisfy the sufficient decrease condition within the maximum allowed iterations, the \textbf{step size is set to zero} to prevent the update and safeguard the solver from adopting an unstable or divergent trajectory.
