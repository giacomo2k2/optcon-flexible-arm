\section{Task 1: Equilibria and Terminal Cost}

\subsection{Equilibrium Manifold}
The system's equilibria are defined by states where $\dot{q} = \ddot{q} = 0$. This implies the gravity and stiffness torques must be balanced by the input $\tau$.
We analyzed the equilibrium manifold by fixing one generalized coordinate ($\theta_1$ or $\theta_2$) and solving for the other.
\begin{equation}
    \cancel{M(\theta_1, \theta_2) \begin{pmatrix} \ddot{\theta}_1 \\ \ddot{\theta}_2 \end{pmatrix}} + \cancel{C(\theta_1, \theta_2, \dot{\theta}_1, \dot{\theta}_2) \begin{pmatrix} \dot{\theta}_1 \\ \dot{\theta}_2 \end{pmatrix}} + \cancel{F \begin{pmatrix} \dot{\theta}_1 \\ \dot{\theta}_2 \end{pmatrix}} + G(\theta_1, \theta_2) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ \tau \end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
This reduces to:
\begin{equation}
    \underbrace{
    \begin{pmatrix} 
    gl_{c1}m_1 \sin(\theta_1) + gm_2(l_1 \sin(\theta_1) + l_{c2} \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)) \\ 
    gm_2l_{c2} \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2) + 3k \sin(\theta_2) \cos^2(\theta_2) 
    \end{pmatrix}
    }_{G(\theta_1, \theta_2)} 
    = 
    \underbrace{
    \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ \tau \end{pmatrix}
    }_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
\end{equation}
In this framework, we have three variables: $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$, and $\tau$. By fixing one of the three variables, we can solve for the other two.
It looks natural, since we are interested in obtaining specific configurations, and since the first joint is actuated, to fix $\theta_1$ and solve for $\theta_2$ and $\tau$.
Using this approach, we generate an equilibrium manifold that is a function of the first joint angle $\theta_1$. It is worth noting that two distinct configurations arise for each $\theta_1$ value, corresponding to different $\theta_2$ angles (e.g., "Elbow-Up" and "Elbow-Down" positions).
They will respectively be stable and unstable equilibria.

    \caption{Equilibrium manifold as a function of $\theta_1$.}
    \label{fig:equilibrium_manifold}
\end{figure}
the first thing we can note is that the manifold has a sinusoidal shape and a periodic behavior, which is expected given the rotational nature of the joints and the influence of gravity.
From the plot, we can also observe some important characteristics:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Branches} We can say that the manifold is constituted by branches, hence there will be starting and ending equilibria that are on the same branch and others that will be in two different ones.\\
This will have an impact on the trajectory generation, as moving between different branches may require more complex maneuvers. 
\item \textbf{Uncontrolled second arm} Since the second joint is unactuated, the torque $\tau$ is only applied at the first joint.\\
This means that the system's ability to reach certain configurations is limited by the dynamics of the second link and the coupling between the two joints.
This coupling will prove useful in achieving a stabilization of the second link by appropriately controlling the first one.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Cost Function}
For the optimal control problem, we defined the quadratic cost function:
\begin{equation}
J = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \left( \frac{1}{2} \Delta x_t^\top Q \Delta x_t + \frac{1}{2} \Delta u_t^\top R \Delta u_t \right) + \frac{1}{2} \Delta x_T^\top Q_T \Delta x_T
\end{equation}
where $\Delta x = x - x_{ref}$ and $\Delta u = u - u_{ref}$. We selected $Q = \text{diag}(1, 1, 1, 1)$ and $R = 10^{-3}I$ to penalize state deviation while allowing for aggressive control actions.

\subsection{Terminal Cost via DARE}
A critical aspect of the finite-horizon optimization is the terminal cost $Q_T$. Simply setting $Q_T = Q$ often fails to stabilize the system at the end of the horizon, especially for short durations.
In fact, without an appropriate terminal cost, the solver may produce trajectories that approach but do not settle at the desired equilibrium, leading to oscillations or drift post-optimization.
It is as if the optimizer result was "myopic", focusing only on minimizing cost up to time $T$ without considering the long-term behavior beyond this point.
To address this, we computed $Q_T$ by solving the \textbf{Discrete Algebraic Riccati Equation} (DARE) based on the linearization at the final target equilibrium $(x_{eq}, u_{eq})$.

We computed $Q_T$ by solving the \textbf{Discrete Algebraic Riccati Equation (DARE)} based on the linearization at the final target equilibrium $(x_{eq}, u_{eq})$.
\begin{equation}
P_{\infty} = A^\top P_{\infty} A - (A^\top P_{\infty} B)(R + B^\top P_{\infty} B)^{-1} (B^\top P_{\infty} A) + Q
\end{equation}
Setting $Q_T = P_{\infty}$ provides an approximation of the infinite-horizon cost-to-go. This essentially informs the solver that the trajectory continues optimally after time $T$, significantly improving convergence and stability for tasks like the "Swing Up".

\subsection{Step Size Selection (Armijo Rule)}
To ensure global convergence of the Newton method, we implemented a line search using the Armijo rule. The step size $\alpha$ is reduced ($\alpha \leftarrow \beta \alpha$) until the actual cost reduction is a sufficient fraction of the predicted reduction (based on the gradient). This prevents the solver from taking overly aggressive steps that could destabilize the trajectory.
